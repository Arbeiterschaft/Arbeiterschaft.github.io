# 为什么我回顾过去时总觉得以前的自己是个傻逼
## Why Do I Always Think My Past Self Was an Idiot?
*2026-11-14, 14：11 EST*

**Read in:** [中文](#chinese) | [English](#english)

## 引言

今天中午吃饭前，我终于下定决心卸载掉手机里的bilibili和小红书。诚然，这两个平台有许多优质的内容，我曾在这里获得知识，信息和乐趣。但是仔细回想，获取真正有用信息的信噪比未免太低。我意识到我每天清醒的时间里有太多浪费在这两个软件上，更可怕的是长期以来的使用习惯似乎已经影响到集中力和注意力，并且我没有控制自己合理使用它们的能力，所以只能出此下策，强迫自己从这种状态中解脱出来。

吃午饭的时候没有视频可看，我只能被迫从一些早已尘封的想法中找出问题来思考。我想起了一个困扰我很久的问题：**为什么我回顾过去的时候总觉得以前的自己是个傻逼？**

## 一个不断重复的后悔周期

在你的人生里，有没有某个瞬间你会希望带着现有的记忆回到过去，寄希望于重新做出选择，抓住错过的机会，掌控自己的人生，改变现在的处境？我曾经多次在某个瞬间突然从自己正在做的事情上抽离出来，回望自己的过往，想起自己没做到和做错的事情，悔之不及。但更严重的是，这种情况就像大梦中短暂的清醒，你痛苦地思考，但并没有真正改变任何事情。浑浑噩噩地生活，突然清醒，反思过去，然后再次浑浑噩噩地活下去。

后悔的内容可以大致分为三种：

**1. 没有提前规划和收集信息，只有远期目标但没有具体实现路径：**

对我而言，这种现象最早发生在高中。当我偶然了解到参加学科竞赛，有机会通过自主招生计划，获得高考的额外加分的时候，班里已经有人早早准备并且有过参与的经验了。我当时就在想，如果我也从初中就开始准备竞赛，我会不会也有机会拿奖？**那时我第一次意识到，有人在明确地规划自己的未来，并为之付出实践。**

高考出分后我才开始研究志愿，随心所欲地选择了两所想去的985和今日回想起来很离谱的专业。殊不知有些朋友早已对人生有了更加清晰的规划：考入优秀的大学，本科期间积累科研经历，来北美读博。最终我去了一所北京本地的211，读通信工程专业。说实话，这个选择是我父母帮我做的，当时的我并不情愿，我本就不想留在北京，分数也足以在别的城市选择985，有段时间总觉得自己明珠暗投。但是福祸相倚，造化弄人，如今回想这个选择，只觉庆幸：不选这个学校，很有可能因为后来的10043禁令和美国说再见；五六年时间过去再回看，当年有多少很正常的专业现在变成了时代的眼泪？**但是人生不能只靠侥幸，虽然做选择的瞬间无法预知结果好坏，但是往往只有把人生抓在自己手里才有做选择的机会。**

**然而即使时至今日，当我可以侃侃而谈我未来想过什么样的生活的时候，我其实依然没有仔细思考过实现那种生活的必要条件。**

**2. 既要又要，朝三暮四，从没有认真把一件事情做到极致**

我可能有一点小聪明，但谈不上有什么突出的天赋。回顾学生时代，学习上放任自流，学习外也没什么建树。一张满分100分的试卷，我觉得自己掌握到90分以后就沾沾自喜，洋洋得意，止步于此，从没有建立起一种追求卓越的习惯。

当然，人不应该把人生完全放在学业上，但是问题在于，不把时间花在学习上，这些时间究竟用来做了什么？

高中时期我喜欢打篮球，花了很多时间练习球技，但是等到大学进了院队，乃至于来到美国以后打球，才发现自己那些练习只是闭门造车，坐井观天；我还花了很多时间玩游戏，比如WoW和Dota2。但是令我后悔的是我并没有真正完全地享受到游戏的乐趣：学习时想着游戏，玩的时候又始终心怀愧疚，最后既没有学出名堂，也没有玩出名堂。**我似乎什么都想做一点，却没有把任何一件事情做到足够好。**

细数这些年所有的爱好：骑车，跑步，登山，书法，音乐，微缩模型，甚至是摩托车，似乎什么都有涉猎，却又都在某个地方停滞不前。

**3. 缺乏执行力，在知道该做什么的时候没有及时去做**

从本科毕业到来美国读master的期间，我有两年时间留在北京，做一些很清闲的工作。现在回头看，这可能是我最遗憾的一段时间。从实现长期人生目标的角度来说，我觉得自己在这两年里几乎没有取得什么实质性的进展。

当然，我在这期间有过几次长途摩旅。这是宝贵的人生经历，我从中积累了人脉，丰富了阅历。但问题在于，如果我真的认为摩旅是一件值得投入的事情，为什么没把更多的时间投入进去？

现在我已经研二了，在Master的第一年，我的重心从嵌入式转到robotics，找到了实验室，有了实习经历，总算积累下来一些能给别人讲出来的成果。但是这学期和一个印度同学接触，我发现自己还做的远远不够。从他身上我学到了很多宝贵的经验，尤其是关于如何主动争取机会。回顾自己Master第一年，我最遗憾的事情之一，就是没有更早地去联系教授、寻找实验室和科研机会。直到第二个学期接近结束时，才主动开口去问，最终很幸运地加入了一个实验室。现在回想起来，为什么没有在刚入学的时候，就去联系十位、二十位教授，了解他们的研究方向，询问是否有适合自己的项目？即使未来不打算读博，这件事情也同样重要，因为我需要真实的项目和科研经历，来支撑我从通信到嵌软再到robotics的转型，这些经历对我而言可以积累真实的技术能力和工程经验。

当然，这种判断可能存在事后偏差，如果没有那两个学期的学习，我未必能在一开始就获得这种机会。但这种不确定性不能完全消除我的遗憾。反过来说，**你不能在没尝试的时候先自己否定自己。**即使尝试过后被别人拒绝，也没有任何的损失，收益期望远大于不采取任何行动。

这也让我更明确了一点：在美国读Master是一件很有意思的事情：很多人支付了相同的学费，最终获得的收获却可能截然不同。学校里的大部分资源，不会主动送到你面前，甚至于这是普遍适用于美国社会的一条行为准则：**有些机会，你主动去问，就可能得到；你不问，它就永远与你无关。**

## 为什么我明明知道什么事情重要，却没有认真去做？

回想这些，再想起Time里那句歌词:
> *And then one day you find, ten years have got behind you.*
*No one told you when to run, you've missed the starting gun.*

很难不悲从中来，觉得自己是个傻逼。

有一种说法是觉得过去的自己傻逼，恰恰说明自己有所长进，曾经我看到这种说法还沾沾自喜。但是后来回过味儿来：GDP还得看增速呢。年龄增长，阅历增加，单纯的长进，但是没有匹配该有的速度，毫无意义。

我也曾数次在高中数学课上幻想过现在的自己带着记忆穿越回少时，是否能重新做出选择，改变曾经的遗憾，变得更聪明？可能我确实是长大了，我意识到这不可能了。**关键点不在于，人不能穿越到过去的自己身上，而在于即使真的能魂穿过去，有些问题一以贯之地存在于我过去的人生中，或者根本就是我自身特质的一部分。不找出它们，改变它们，我不会发生根本变化。**

刚才列举的遗憾，我觉得可以具体指向三个问题：

**1. 为什么对自己要求很低？**
坦白来说，我觉得这不是一个能改变的问题，**我就是这么个人，差不多得了。**这个问题只能落到实处：比如说做一个实验，提前把数据的标准定好；写一份报告前，先把所有标准确定了再严格执行。提前把自己得过且过的退路赌上，逼自己更'professional'一点。

**2. 为什么缺乏长期规划？**
为什么只有在某个阶段结束以后，才意识到曾经存在过更好的路径？**为什么有些人可以以当局者的身份看破迷雾提前做规划？**

回顾自身，我发现我是拥有欲望的，我的人生中其实总是有着长期目标，它们也一直随着人生状态更新而变化。但是我很少思考如何去具体实现这个长期目标。这是一个非常复杂的问题，思考这种问题注定是痛苦的。就像我做过的每一个大型项目一样，我在初期往往选择逃避思考，在实在逃避不掉的时候选择跟随本能行动，在ddl前爆气干出绝大部分内容，但最后的质量往往不及预期。

另一个原因是信息收集能力。打DOTA最重要的是视野，制定合理的计划必须依靠准确的信息。我认为自己在使用互联网工具收集信息的能力上不存在太大的问题，这部分无非是到底有没有做，投入了多少时间的差距。但信息收集的另一个来源我认为我做的很差:和人聊天。
在没有电子设备和网络的年代，口口相传是信息传播的主要方式之一。在学校里，和学长学姐以及老师聊天，再到北美的networking文化，直接约陌生的校友/企业员工进行coffee chat，本质上都是在刷存在感和通过对话获得网络上没有的信息。我有和别人对话的决心和勇气，但是我确实不擅长通过对话获得信息。这个别无他法，没有天赋，只能靠堆量去练习了。

**3. 为什么缺乏执行力？**
这是拖延症。但是拖延的过程到底是什么?我为什么会拖延某些任务?

首先，我拖延的主要是那些重要，复杂，但又没有明确ddl，或周期很长的任务。思考这种任务有点痛苦，不如先做后天就要交的作业吧，带着这种想法，这些大任务往往被推迟到再不开始就真的寄了，我才忍着痛苦上手做。这个问题似乎从小就伴随着我: 睡觉之前肯定要玩和写作业，但我从来没有先写过作业。

一定要改变这一点，我决定劝自己: **如果吃屎不能避免，还是先把屎吃了再吃别的吧。**

其次，是高强度使用手机刷视频给我带来的后遗症: 我好像无法长时间集中注意力，在做一件事的过程中不自觉地拿起手机开始刷，或者通过一直刷手机老逃避开始做一件事情，想鸵鸟把头扎进地里，仿佛那个任务不存在。这个问题太过于可怕了，并且我尝试过很多次也没有能真正把自己纠正过来。说实话，这很明显是一种成瘾表现，我这次下大决心要戒断。工作和睡觉的时候和手机物理隔离，删掉常用的app是我目前的解决方案，先尝试一段时间看看，注意力问题可能还需要别的方法去恢复。

## 我怎样才能让未来的自己不再重复今天的后悔？

如前文所言，这种思考其实并不是第一次，但真正认真记录下来还是首次。并且在记录的过程中随着不断地思考，也涌现出了的新问题和解决办法。并且我思考出了能把很多问题统一起来的一种解决办法:

**学习一种科学合理地制定计划的方法。**

我认为这可以同时解决缺乏长期计划和拖延的问题。首先，这个方法可以通过可复制的流程，分解长期目标和大型任务的目标，制定可执行的计划，解决我空有目标没有计划的迷茫状态；其次，我可以通过细分计划的步骤，得到明确的小型阶段性任务，让屎看起来没那么难吃，以此来对抗拖延。

在没有调查这种方法之前，先留下我对它的思考:
我曾经受到过Sung教授的启发: 我们聊到想要发一篇论文，首先就根据截稿时间和研究领域先选择期刊或者会议，再对应刊物要求和偏好，去讨论最终成果应该是什么样的。然后对应这个具象化的成果，反推都需要什么技术和证明实验，最后逐步确认实验计划和时间表。

类比一下，如果是找工作的话: 首先需要了解岗位在US的具体情况，比如人数和薪资；然后需要明确这个岗位需要什么技术，不同公司有什么差别；如何丰富经历或者根据对应岗位要求制作简历，如何投递简历，如何准备技术面试等等。

我对此的总结是:**制定目标明确的战略方向和结合局势的战术内容。**

但是依然需要去调查一下什么是科学合理地制定计划的方法。有个很典型的例子就是社会主义国家计划建设的各种五年计划。我希望真的能从中找到或者总结出解决这些问题的方法论。

## 结语

本来我想用 "*悟已往之不谏，知来者之可追*" 来劝勉自己作为结尾，但我突然想起来每次我思考这个问题都用这句话结束思考，根本不是劝勉，充其量只是宽慰。

所以我决定换一种方式。我在文中提到曾多次幻想魂穿过去，但却从没试想过反过来:
**如果我突然魂穿到2028年深秋的自己，我那时会过着什么样的生活?我应该如何评价这过去的两年时间?**

我希望睁开眼我能看到落日缓缓沉入海平面，太平洋微暖的海风拂面，身边不再是现在*CRF300*，而是一辆*T700*或者*Africa Twin* 

**希望那时的我回顾这两年，会觉得自己确实有所改变，没再像以前那样虚度时间。不管命运是眷顾我，还是时运不济命途多舛，我都能坦然接它的安排。**

# Why Do I Always Think My Past Self Was an Idiot?

*2026-11-14, 14：11 EST*

## Introduction

Before lunch today, I finally made up my mind to uninstall Bilibili and Xiaohongshu from my phone. To be fair, both platforms have plenty of high-quality content. I've learned things, found useful information, and had a lot of fun on them. But looking back, the signal-to-noise ratio is just ridiculously low. I've realized that far too much of my waking time is spent on these two apps. What's even more frightening is that years of using them seem to have affected my ability to concentrate and pay attention. And since I apparently lack the self-control to use them in moderation, I had to resort to this drastic measure to force myself out of this situation.

With no videos to watch over lunch, I was forced to dig up some long-buried thoughts and find something to think about. That's when I remembered a question that has bothered me for a long time: **Why do I always think my past self was an idiot whenever I look back?**

## An Endless Cycle of Regret

Have you ever had a moment in your life when you wished you could go back in time with all your current memories? To make different choices, seize the opportunities you missed, take control of your own life, and change where you are today?

I've had many moments when I suddenly stepped back from whatever I was doing and looked at my past, remembering all the things I failed to do and all the mistakes I made, overwhelmed with regret. But what's even worse is that these moments feel like briefly waking up in the middle of a long dream. You think painfully about your life, yet nothing really changes. **You drift through life, suddenly wake up, reflect on the past, and then go right back to drifting through life.**

The things I regret can roughly be divided into three categories.

**1. Failing to Plan Ahead and Gather Information: Having Long-Term Goals but No Concrete Path to Achieve Them**

For me, this first happened in high school. When I happened to learn that participating in academic Olympiads could give students a chance to earn bonus points on the Gaokao through special university admission programs, some of my classmates had already been preparing for years and even had competition experience. I remember thinking: If I had started preparing for these competitions back in middle school, would I have had a chance to win something too? **That was the first time I realized that some people were consciously planning their futures and actually taking steps toward them.**

After my Gaokao results came out, I only then started researching universities and majors. I casually picked two Project 985 universities I wanted to attend, along with some majors that seem absolutely ridiculous to me now. Little did I know that some of my friends had already mapped out much clearer paths for their lives: get into a prestigious university, build research experience during their undergraduate years, and then pursue a PhD in North America.

In the end, I attended a local Project 211 university in Beijing, majoring in Communications Engineering. To be honest, that decision was largely made by my parents. I wasn't particularly happy about it. I had never wanted to stay in Beijing, and my Gaokao score was high enough to get me into a Project 985 university in another city. For a while, I felt like a pearl thrown into the wrong place.

**But fortune and misfortune are intertwined, and fate has a strange way of working.** Looking back now, I feel nothing but grateful for that choice. Had I attended a different university, Presidential Proclamation 10043 might very well have put an end to my plans to study in the United States. And looking back five or six years later, how many majors that seemed perfectly reasonable at the time have now become casualties of a changing era?

**But you can't build your life on luck alone. Even though you can never know whether a decision will turn out well or badly at the moment you make it, you usually have to take your life into your own hands before you even get the chance to make meaningful choices.**

**And yet, even today, when I can talk confidently about the kind of life I want to live in the future, I still haven't seriously thought through what it would actually take to get there.**

**2. Wanting Everything, Constantly Changing Directions, and Never Truly Committing to Doing One Thing Exceptionally Well**

I might have been a little clever, but I certainly never had any outstanding talent. Looking back at my school years, I mostly coasted through my studies, and I didn't accomplish much outside the classroom either. On a test worth 100 points, once I felt I had mastered enough material to score 90, I would become smug and satisfied with myself and stop there. I never developed the habit of striving for excellence.

Of course, nobody should devote their entire life to academics. **But here's the question: if I wasn't spending my time studying, what exactly was I doing with it?**

In high school, I loved basketball and spent a lot of time practicing. But when I joined my department's basketball team in college, and later played basketball in the United States, I realized how limited my perspective had been. All that practice had been like working behind closed doors, completely unaware of how much more there was to learn.

I also spent countless hours playing games like World of Warcraft and Dota 2. But what I regret is that I never fully enjoyed gaming either. When I was studying, I wanted to play games. When I was gaming, I felt guilty about not studying. In the end, I neither achieved much academically nor got particularly good at the games I played.

It seems I wanted to do a little bit of everything, but never managed to do any one thing particularly well. Looking at all the hobbies I've had over the years—cycling, running, hiking, calligraphy, music, miniature painting, and even motorcycling—it seems I've tried a bit of everything, only to hit a plateau somewhere along the way.

**3. Lacking the Ability to Act: Not Doing Things When I Already Knew I Should**

Between graduating from college and coming to the United States for my master's degree, I spent two years in Beijing doing relatively undemanding work. Looking back, this might be the period of my life I regret the most. In terms of moving toward my long-term goals, I feel like I accomplished almost nothing substantial during those two years.

Of course, I did go on several long-distance motorcycle trips. Those were valuable life experiences. I made connections and broadened my horizons. But if I really believed motorcycle touring was something worth pursuing, why didn't I devote more time to it?

Now I'm in the second year of my master's program. During my first year, I shifted my focus from embedded systems to robotics, joined a research lab, gained internship experience, and finally accumulated some achievements I could actually talk about. But after getting to know an Indian classmate this semester, I realized I was still nowhere near doing enough. I've learned a lot of valuable things from him, especially about how to actively seek out opportunities.

Looking back at my first year of graduate school, one of my biggest regrets is not reaching out to professors and looking for research opportunities sooner. It wasn't until near the end of my second semester that I finally took the initiative to ask around, and fortunately, I managed to join a lab. Now I can't help wondering: **why didn't I contact ten or twenty professors as soon as I arrived? Why didn't I learn about their research interests and ask whether they had any projects I could join?**

Even if I don't plan to pursue a PhD, this would still have been incredibly important. I need real projects and research experience to support my transition from communications engineering to embedded software and eventually to robotics. These experiences would help me develop genuine technical skills and engineering expertise.

Of course, I realize this judgment may be influenced by hindsight bias. Without those two semesters of coursework, I might not have been qualified to get such an opportunity in the first place. But that uncertainty doesn't completely erase my regret. Look at it the other way: **you shouldn't reject yourself before you've even tried.** Even if someone turns you down after you ask, you've lost practically nothing. The potential payoff is far greater than doing nothing at all.

This has also made one thing much clearer to me: There's something interesting about pursuing a master's degree in the United States. Many students pay the same tuition, yet what they ultimately get out of the experience can be completely different. Most of the resources a university offers won't simply be handed to you. In fact, I think this reflects a broader principle of American society:

**Some opportunities become yours simply because you ask. If you never ask, they will never have anything to do with you.**

## Why Don't I Take Things Seriously Even When I Know They're Important?

Thinking back on all of this, I can't help remembering those lines from Pink Floyd's *Time*:

> *And then one day you find, ten years have got behind you.*
*No one told you when to run, you've missed the starting gun.*

It's hard not to feel overwhelmed with sadness and think: what a fucking idiot I've been.

There's a popular saying that if you look back and think your past self was an idiot, it simply means you've grown. I used to feel pretty pleased with myself whenever I heard that. But eventually, I started thinking about it differently: even GDP is judged by its growth rate. As you get older, you naturally gain more experience and learn a few things. But if you're not growing at the pace you should be, what does that growth really mean?

Back in high school, I used to daydream during math class about traveling back in time with all my current memories. Would I be able to make different choices, avoid my old regrets, and become a smarter person? Maybe I really have grown up, because I've finally realized that this is impossible.

**But the real point isn't that I can't travel back in time. It's that even if I could somehow send my consciousness back into my younger body, some of these problems have been present throughout my entire life. They might even be part of who I am. Unless I identify them and change them, nothing fundamental will change.**

Looking at all the regrets I've listed, I think they point to three specific problems.

**1. Why Do I Set Such Low Standards for Myself?**

Frankly, I don't think this is something I can change. **This is just the kind of person I am. Good enough is good enough.** So instead of trying to change my personality, I need to address this problem in practical ways.

For example, before running an experiment, I should define the standards the data need to meet. Before writing a report, I should establish its structure and then follow it strictly. In other words, I need to close off my usual escape route of settling for "good enough" before I even begin, forcing myself to be a little more professional.

**2. Why Do I Lack Long-Term Planning?**

Why do I only realize that better paths existed after a certain stage of life has already ended? **Why are some people able to see through the fog and plan ahead while they're still in the middle of it?**

Looking back at myself, I realize that I do have desires and ambitions. I've always had long-term goals, and those goals have evolved as my circumstances have changed. But I rarely think seriously about how to actually achieve them.

That's an incredibly complicated problem, and thinking about complicated problems is inevitably painful. It's just like every major project I've ever worked on. At the beginning, I usually avoid thinking too deeply about it. When I can no longer avoid it, I start acting on instinct. Then, right before the deadline, I suddenly go into overdrive and complete most of the work in one desperate burst. And the final quality is usually worse than I expected.

Another factor is the ability to gather information. In Dota, vision is everything. And making a reasonable plan requires accurate information. I don't think I have much of a problem using internet tools to gather information. For me, it's mostly a matter of whether I actually do the research and how much time I put into it.

But there's another source of information that I think I'm terrible at using: **Talking to people.**

Before electronic devices and the internet, word of mouth was one of the main ways information spread. At university, talking to older students and professors serves a similar purpose. Then there's the networking culture in North America, where people directly reach out to alumni or employees they've never met and ask for a coffee chat. At their core, these are all ways of making yourself known and obtaining information that isn't available online.

I have the determination and courage to talk to people, but I'm genuinely not very good at extracting useful information from conversations. There's no shortcut here. If I don't have a natural talent for it, I'll just have to practice through sheer repetition.

**3. Why Do I Lack the Ability to Get Things Done?**

This is *procrastination*. But what exactly happens when I procrastinate? Why do I put certain tasks off?

First of all, the tasks I tend to procrastinate on are usually important and complicated, but have no clear deadline or have a very long timeline. Thinking about these tasks is painful. So I tell myself: why not finish the assignment that's due the day after tomorrow first?

With that mindset, major tasks keep getting pushed back until I'm practically screwed if I don't start immediately. Only then do I finally force myself to tackle them. This problem seems to have followed me since childhood. When I was a kid, I knew I had two things to do before bed: play and finish my homework. **But I never once chose to do my homework first.**

I absolutely have to change this. So I've decided to tell myself:

**If eating shit is unavoidable, I might as well eat the shit first and enjoy everything else afterward.**

Second, there's the damage caused by my excessive habit of scrolling through videos on my phone. I seem to have lost the ability to concentrate on something for a long time. In the middle of doing something, I'll unconsciously pick up my phone and start scrolling. Or I'll keep scrolling to avoid starting a task in the first place, like an ostrich burying its head in the sand, pretending the task doesn't exist.

This is terrifying, especially because I've tried many times to correct this behavior and never truly succeeded. To be honest, this is clearly a form of addiction, and this time I'm determined to quit. Physically separating myself from my phone while working and sleeping, and deleting the apps I use most frequently, are the solutions I've come up with so far. I'll try them for a while and see what happens. As for restoring my attention span, I may need to find other methods.

## How Can I Stop My Future Self from Repeating the Same Regrets?

As I mentioned earlier, this isn't the first time I've thought about these problems. But it is the first time I've seriously written them down. And as I kept writing and thinking, new questions and possible solutions continued to emerge. Eventually, I came up with one idea that might address several of these problems at once:

**Learn a scientific and systematic way to make plans.**

I believe this could help me solve both my lack of long-term planning and my tendency to procrastinate. First, a repeatable process could help me break down long-term goals and major projects into concrete, actionable plans, solving the problem of having ambitions but no idea how to achieve them. Second, by dividing a plan into smaller steps, I could create clear, manageable tasks that make the shit look a little less disgusting, helping me fight procrastination.

Before I actually research these methods, I want to write down my initial thoughts. I was once inspired by something Professor Sung taught me. We were discussing how to publish a research paper. The first step was to choose a journal or conference based on its submission deadline and research area. Then, based on the venue's requirements and preferences, we would discuss what the final research contribution should look like. Starting from that concrete outcome, we would work backward to identify the necessary techniques and experiments needed to support the claims. Finally, we would gradually establish an experimental plan and timeline.

The same logic could apply to finding a job. First, I need to understand the actual job market in the United States: how many positions are available, what they pay, and so on. Then I need to identify the technical skills required for those positions and how the requirements differ across companies. After that, I need to figure out how to strengthen my experience, tailor my resume to specific roles, apply for jobs, prepare for technical interviews, and so forth.

My summary of this approach is:

**Establish a clear strategic direction, then develop tactical plans based on the actual situation.**

But I still need to investigate what a truly scientific and reasonable planning method looks like. One obvious example is the various Five-Year Plans used by socialist countries for economic and national development. I hope I can genuinely find, or develop for myself, a methodology that helps solve these problems.
